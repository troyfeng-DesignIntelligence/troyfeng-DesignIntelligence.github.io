An essay, 2026

Visual Branding and the Politics of Gentrification

Barcelona through Three Urban Mirrors

When I first arrived in Barcelona, the city seemed unusually aware of its own image. Its pavements, façades, monuments, shopfronts, housing photographs and museum narratives form a visual system that makes the city easy to recognise and consume. This essay asks what that system does to urban belonging.

01The branded city

A grid ready for the camera.

I treat city branding not as a logo or a campaign but as a distributed spatial interface: a set of visual and sensory cues that organises who and what becomes visible. In Barcelona the 1992 Olympics gave it political will and the Eixample grid a perfect canvas. Walking the city every day as an international student, I read it in shopfronts, streets, rented rooms and queues.

  • The boutique-ification of necessityThe ferretería, the cheap copy shop and the bakery give way to specialty coffee and concept stores: a visual upgrade that can signal a functional loss.
  • Sensory displacementLighting turns brighter and whiter; neighbours’ voices give way to the hum of commerce and the rumble of suitcase wheels on cobblestones.
  • Housing as a visual assetRooms for students and mobile professionals are made to be consumed through photographs: optimised for turnover, not dwelling.
  • The architectural cityAt Casa Batlló the queue and the gaze occupy the pavement, and the story of Modernisme casts other histories into shadow.

02Three mirrors

Cities as mirrors, not matching cases.

The comparison is asymmetrical on purpose. I do not compare Barcelona, Beijing, Shanghai and Tallinn in their entirety, but specific visual operations: what happens to inconvenient histories, essential residential spaces, preserved architecture and obsolete monuments.

  1. BarcelonaSustained everyday experience: months of walking as an international student.
  2. Huilongguan, BeijingFour focused site visits in September 2024, two there and two to Beijing Pixel, a point of reference rather than a fifth case.
  3. Zhangyuan, ShanghaiA documented heritage-redevelopment case, read from public sources rather than fieldwork.
  4. Linnahall, TallinnA direct visit in December 2025, with historical and current planning sources.

03Four regimes

Visibility is an operation, not a ranking.

Cities do not only hide inconvenient realities. They may preserve them as heritage, keep them at a distance, retain the architecture while replacing its use, or leave places unresolved.

Absorption

MUHBA Bon Pastor, Barcelona

A preserved block of cases barates, cheap houses built in 1929, tells the history of working-class housing. The city expands its official image by incorporating a marginal history, and recognition arrives once the houses are no longer lived in as they once were.

Separation

Huilongguan, Beijing

The everyday city is not destroyed or curated into heritage; it is placed at a physical and symbolic distance from the city’s most circulated image. Workers commute inward, labour and return outward.

Programmatic overwriting

Zhangyuan, Shanghai

Preservation itself becomes a technology of change. The brick, lane names, doors and decorative surfaces remain legible while the programme of ordinary collective dwelling is largely rewritten.

Suspension

Linnahall, Tallinn

Built for the sailing events of the 1980 Moscow Olympics, the concrete complex is visible but unresolved, and not yet fully put to work for the city brand.

No regime is pure. Bon Pastor can support memory while turning struggle into heritage; Huilongguan is not invisible to residents or planning; Zhangyuan improved many homes while removing domestic life from the site; Linnahall’s ambiguity can preserve openness and produce abandonment.

04The right to the image

A city easier to consume than to inhabit.

Visual coherence is never merely aesthetic. It works as soft governance, shaping who is invited to look, who is allowed to stay, and whose everyday life can appear in the city’s image.

A truly open city is not one that looks perfect in a photograph. It is one that allows itself to be messy, unfinished, and continually rewritten by the people who live there.

Can a city have a strong visual identity without excluding those who do not fit the picture?

What this record can't say

  • It is an autoethnography: one international student’s walks and readings, not a study of residents. No participants were recruited and no dataset was made.
  • The comparison is asymmetrical by design: Barcelona from everyday life, Huilongguan from four visits in September 2024, Linnahall from one visit in December 2025, Zhangyuan from documents only.
  • The four regimes are analytical terms derived from situated encounters, not universal city types.
  • The Huilongguan and Zhangyuan diagrams are analytical readings, not demographic measurements.
  • It is a preprint, not a peer-reviewed publication.
