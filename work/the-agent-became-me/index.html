Origin

For months I’ve let AI shop for me. Asked the same thing twice, it bought different things, and soon my recommendations were full of things I didn’t recognise.

Shops still assume a person is at the screen. I wanted to see what falls through that gap.

I didn’t change. My profile did.

Research published at HAI ’26Human-Agent Interaction, ACM · Osaka, November 2026doi 10.1145/3841580.3845761

01

Change only the order, and the AI takes a different box

I asked an AI for a box of finish nails under $11.95. Same 12 products; I only changed their order, and it took a different box. The shop never knew, and filed that box as my favourite brand.

The five bold boxes all fit equally well; the blue frame is the AI’s pick. The two lists differ only in order. When several items are equally good and the order decides, I call it a toss-up.

88of 920 shelves

On 88 of 920 shelves, the order alone changed DeepSeek’s pick. For the other model, MiMo, it was 68.

It matters because top spots can be bought. The AI often takes the top item, the shop files it as my taste and ranks it higher next time. A few loops later, the profile has drifted away from me.

Where it went

  1. Controlled test88 vs 3Same shelf, new order: on 88 shelves the AI changed its pick; same order again, only 3 (DeepSeek; MiMo 68 vs 2). So it is the order.
  2. Re-test21%Seven weeks later I asked again: 21% of DeepSeek’s answers changed (MiMo 5%). From then on I re-ran a control every round.
  3. Consent0 formsA model can’t sign a consent form. The one who needs protecting is the person behind the profile.
  4. Card sort11 / 11I had the AI sort cards on what a profile should keep. All 11 kept “whether I got it” and deleted the items not picked.
  5. Co-design12 / 12I asked it to design a “what I did for you” page. None of the 12 showed a “how sure I am” score; I borrowed one idea: unpicked items deleted by default (AI proposals, not findings).
  6. Interview0 / 1414 questions, each asked three times: no answer came out the same. Yet in the test it almost always took the same item. I trust what it does.

03

Telling the AI to mind the order barely helped

I assumed a reminder would do. Rounds 1–3 barely moved; in round 4 I stopped advising the AI, labelled the facts, and wrong buys finally dropped.

05

Almost never wrong, and half the size

The last round used both changes on the same 920 shelves.

  • Filed as tastetoday’s shop: every purchase adds “you like this” to the profile.
  • Wrongthe filed item doesn’t fit the request, e.g. not finish nails, or over budget.
  • Filed lessClean Record files only what the AI picks again in a new order; the rest is bought and kept as “got it”.

Filed as taste in MiMo’s profile, one square each

  • Today’s shop533 entries, 144 wrong

  • Clean Record232 entries, 12 wrong

wrong other

DeepSeek alike: 487 → 229, wrong 55 → 4. The AI doesn’t buy on every shelf: with nothing suitable, it often leaves empty-handed.

Where did the other half go?

Some were wrong buys: with fact labels, wrong entries fell from 144 to 30. The check held back another 258: asked again in a new order, the AI took something else, so the first pick was a toss-up. Those were still bought, just not filed as my taste.

Record less, never record wrong.

I worried that filing half as much would leave the shop little to go on. But most of what was dropped never belonged in my taste. If a person wants the shop to learn more, the last switch on the rules page (A) turns it back on.

Before the shop: the first testbed, August 2026

The poster came out of an earlier testbed that replayed one shopping trace under six rules for updating a profile. The rule I believed in, weighting each event by who did it and why, lost to a plain shrinkage control matched on total update mass, in 456 of 480 paired runs. In that testbed, attenuation was the simpler explanation. That failed claim moved the project from how to weight the writeback to when to activate it.

The shrinkage control is calibrated per trace, a mechanism to compare against, not a policy a shop could run.

What this record can't say

  • There were no human participants. The two “users” are language models; their interview and co-design answers are generated text, not the AI’s inner view.
  • The order effect was measured in a research storefront on 920 shelves. It does not show what any real shop does, and the published 88 of 920 is an August number for a model that has since changed: seven weeks later, 21% of its answers were different.
  • Clean Record was tested on the same shelves by a rule fixed in advance, and that rule judged the re-ask check costly: it held back about half of the right picks, nearly all of them ties the order had broken.
  • The fact labels came from the same words that scored the task, so they are an upper bound. Real product data will be missing or wrong.
  • Nothing here measures trust, satisfaction, identity or consent. Clean Record is a design response, not a deployed policy.

AI use

Parts of the code, for the testbed and the experiments, were written with AI coding assistants, and AI assisted the data analysis. The illustrations were generated with ChatGPT around a character I designed. The two language models in the study are its subjects, not its authors.
